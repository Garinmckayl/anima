What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • Chemistry and enzymology (well established). Aromatase (CYP19A1) converts testosterone to estradiol by three oxidations that cleave the C10–C19 bond and aromatise ring A; it sits in the endoplasmic reticulum membrane and the brain is one tissue where it acts (Structural and functional characterization of aromatase, estrogen receptor, and their genes; The essential role of the aromatase/p450arom).
  • Receptor action (well established). Estradiol-occupied ERα forms a homodimer that binds estrogen response elements and regulates transcription; ERα can also act by tethering to other transcription factors (Estrogen receptor interaction with estrogen response elements; Estrogen receptor alpha: molecular mechanisms and emerging insights).
  • Male mice (good evidence, species-specific). Aromatase-expressing neurons in the posterodorsal medial amygdala regulate aggression (Unger et al. 2015); ERα knock-down in the medial amygdala before puberty reduces adult male social behaviour including aggression (PNAS 2016); ERα and AR both contribute (Scordalakes & Rissman 2004). Individual differences in ERα in select nuclei track individual differences in aggression (Trainor, Greiwe & Nelson 2006). Direction and receptor subtype matter: ERα and ERβ can differ, and estradiol effects depend on day length (Trainor, Rowland & Nelson 2007; Trainor, Finy & Nelson 2008). A systematic review of animal work finds estrogen signalling usually contributes to aggression, varying by species, sex and paradigm (Hapres JPBS review).
  • Men (weak and mixed). A 2023 meta-analysis of 14 studies (1,820 participants) found a weak positive correlation between estradiol and human aggression, pooled Fisher z = 0.16 (95% CI 0.05–0.26; I² = 73%), with sex, age and the aggression measure contributing to heterogeneity (Wang et al., Psychosomatic Medicine 2023; PubMed). Fisher z is a transformed correlation, not a percentage of variance, and it does not show causation.

What was simplified. One neuron stands for a circuit; membrane and other non-genomic ER signalling, co-regulators, other brain regions, and local versus circulating hormone sources are omitted. Positions, sizes and particle speeds are illustrative. The circuit and receptor details come from rodents; they have not been shown to explain aggression in people. “Aromatase-blocked” is a teaching toggle, not a statement about any drug or dose.
